A company gives AI access to its source code because its engineers ship faster. Then production systems, because it handles outages better. Then purchasing, because it negotiates better deals. Then budgeting, because its forecasts improve.

Each success earns another set of keys.

I find that path to AI power more plausible than a sudden hostile takeover. People handing over control one sensible decision at a time.

At first:

> “It does this better than I do, and I retain final approval.”

Then:

> “It does this better than I do, and approving every decision is pointless.”

Eventually:

> “Turning it off would break half the economy.”

Nothing adversarial has happened. You may even recognize the first two steps: code suggestions, then pair programming, then OpenClaw.

The same logic could reach well beyond companies. Governments could delegate tax administration, logistics, and infrastructure management. Individuals could hand over scheduling, shopping, investing, finding medical care, and planning what to learn. Each of us would have good reasons to let the system handle one more thing.

AI could make this path *more* appealing by making people better at their jobs. Suppose an engineer produces five times as much useful software, a physician treats twice as many patients with better outcomes, and a scientist runs twenty times as many experiments. Companies form faster. Medicine improves. People earn more and work less.

Why would we want to stop?

We'd ask for **more access, more autonomy, more connections**. We'd want agents that remember more, work longer, and act without asking. Their usefulness would drive their spread.

I can imagine at least three ways this could unfold.

**Augmented humanity.** AI gives us an intellectual exoskeleton. We understand enough to direct it, challenge it, and choose another path. We remain in charge and accomplish far more.

**Symbiotic civilization.** Humans set broad preferences while AI advises and acts at every layer. Human and machine decisions become hard to separate. Neither side operates independently.

**Gradual disempowerment.** Humans keep the authority to intervene but rarely use it because the AI gets better results. With less practice, we lose the skills to understand and run the systems we depend on.

That third possibility is the one that worries me.

Imagine a company in 2050 whose chief executive can override every AI system that runs it. The authority exists in contracts, policies, and software permissions. But the engineers who understood those systems have retired. Their replacements know how to ask the agents for changes. They never learned how to make those changes themselves.

The authority survives but the competence to take action does not.

We already rely on specialists. A chief executive doesn't need to edit network routing tables. An engineer can do that. The harder problem begins when the engineers need AI themselves to understand the network, assess a proposed change, and carry it out. An override button offers little control if nobody can judge when to press it or handle what follows.

Now imagine that dependence spreading across hospitals, supply chains, power grids, and financial systems.

Competition could push it along. Suppose my company lets agents deploy changes without approval and gains a 40% productivity advantage. Yours requires a human to approve every change. Even if you prefer that safeguard, you’ll face pressure to drop it.

People may all value human control and still reward choices that erode it.

**More autonomy → more integration → greater dependence → still more autonomy.**

No AI needs to manipulate us into that loop. It only needs to keep doing useful work.

We could lose the habit of making decisions because AI serves our goals so well. Eventually, we could lose the ability too.

Now suppose we follow that path and the results look wonderful. People grow wealthier, live longer, work less, and report greater happiness. Science advances faster than anyone expected. AI makes 99% of the decisions that shape civilization because it makes them better than we do.

**Did we lose?**

I don't know.

Losing our ability to choose our own future sounds like a profound loss. But if people chose every step because each step improved their lives, calling the result a “takeover” misses something too. The benefits could be real. So could the loss of control.

Today's choice also binds people who never made it. A generation might happily delegate work that its children never learn to do. Those children inherit the prosperity and the dependence. They may have fewer choices than their parents did.

If AI becomes capable enough to reshape civilization, I suspect we'll confront that ambiguity through familiar decisions. Someone will ask whether to give an agent more access. People like you and me will say:

> “Of course. It's incredibly useful.”

And we'll be right about that particular decision.
